The honest version: if you've already interviewed your customer and you just need a skilled writer, a good freelancer is a fine call and a lot cheaper than me. If you want to type "write me a case study" into ChatGPT and ship whatever comes out, please don't. The thing that separates a case study that convinces from content that gets ignored is that it's **real**: a real customer, on the record, on camera. AI can't give you that, and a freelancer only gives you the writing half of it.

I know exactly how good that route can be and exactly where it falls over.

## What ChatGPT and a good freelancer are genuinely good at

Let me concede the obvious, because it's real.

They're cheaper. They're faster. And you keep full control the whole way through. You're not waiting on my calendar or my production schedule. You can have a draft in front of you in the next ten minutes if you want one.

For a freelancer specifically, the ceiling is higher than people give it credit for. A genuinely good freelance writer can produce a case study that's every bit as sharp as anything a studio ships. I've seen freelancer-written case studies with a tight narrative, real quotes, and specific numbers that I'd have been proud to put my name on. The best of them are excellent, and they charge a fraction of what a studio does. No shade. Some of my best mates are freelancers, and I was one for years.

The catch with freelancers is *variance*. A good one is genuinely excellent, but you're betting on the individual, and there's no system behind them. I've also seen freelancer case studies that read like the writer googled "case study template" ten minutes before they started. Same platform, same brief, wildly different result. When it's good it's very good. When it's bad you've lost your money and your time, and you often can't tell which one you've bought until it lands.

ChatGPT is a different animal, and I want to be fair about what it's actually good at too. It's a brilliant drafting tool. It'll give you a structure, tidy up your own writing, suggest headings, unstick you when you're staring at a blank page. If you've done the interview yourself and you've got a transcript full of real quotes, feeding that to an AI to help shape a first draft is completely legitimate. That's using it as a tool.

The problem is what happens when people use it as the *whole process*.

## Where the Proof Kit wins: it's real, and it's produced

Here's the thing AI physically cannot do. It can't get on a call with your customer.

Which means it has no real quotes. No numbers your customer actually said out loud and stood behind. No moment where the customer describes the exact problem they had before they found you, in their words, in a way your next buyer reads and thinks "that's me". AI either invents the metrics, which is a credibility problem and potentially a legal one, or it reaches for filler like "streamlined their workflow and saw significant improvement in efficiency". You've read a thousand of those sentences. So has your buyer. They mean nothing.

And 2026 buyers have their radar up. [94% of buyers now use LLMs somewhere in their buying process](https://6sense.com/science-of-b2b/buyer-experience-report-2025/), which means they're staring at AI-generated text all day and they've got very good at spotting it. There's a real irony here that nobody talks about. A case study exists to prove that real humans use your product and get real results. If the case study itself was obviously written by a machine, what exactly have you proved?

There's a search cost too. Google's helpful content system is built to spot and bury pages that exist to game search rather than help a person. So the free option can end up invisible, which is its own kind of expensive.

## "I'll just use ChatGPT" / "a freelancer's way cheaper"

You will, and it is. Both true. I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

The question isn't whether the cheap route is cheaper. It obviously is. The question is what your proof is *for*. It exists to convince a sceptical buyer who's about to spend real money and is looking for a reason to believe you or a reason to bail. If that buyer reads your case study and thinks "this feels generic" or "did a bot write this?", it's done the opposite of its job. You didn't just get nothing. You spent your credibility making yourself look less credible.

That's the trap with the DIY floor. When the output's bad, it's bad in the one way that actively costs you the deal. And you usually can't tell from the inside, because it looks fine to you. You wrote it, or you briefed it, so of course it reads okay to you. Your buyer is the one who decides, and they're a lot harder to fool than your own eye.

## So who should pick what?

I'll be straight, because sending some of you elsewhere is the whole point of writing this honestly.

**Use ChatGPT** to draft, to structure, to tidy your own writing, or to get unstuck. Just don't let it be the finished, published thing, and never let it invent a number. If you've run the interview yourself and you've got real quotes to work from, AI is a fine pair of hands for the first draft. Do the human part first.

**Hire a freelancer** if you've done the interview (or you'll do it yourself), you know what a good case study should say, and you just need skilled writing hands. That's a completely sensible spend, and a good one will do a lovely job for a few hundred quid. Stress-test "good" before you commit, because there's nothing behind them but their own taste. Ask for samples, and read them like your buyer would.
