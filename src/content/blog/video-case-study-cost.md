A video case study will cost you somewhere between **$0 and $10,000+ per video**. Here's the quick breakdown:

- **DIY (phone and Zoom):** $0–$300.
- **Self-serve collection software:** $0–$249/mo.
- **Freelance videographer:** roughly $1,000–$4,000 per finished video.
- **Productised studio:** $2,500 fixed.
- **Premium video agency:** $6,510–$54,000/yr on annual credits, roughly $3,000+ per on-site video.
- **Full-service production house:** $10,000+ per video, usually on retainer.

I run [Best Case Studio](/), and we sit in the productised tier at $2,500, so read this knowing where my money comes from. There's a separate breakdown for the written side ([how much a B2B case study costs](/blog/how-much-does-a-b2b-case-study-cost/)), so this one stays on video. Every published figure below comes from the company's own pricing page. Where a number is an estimate, I say so and I say why.

## What does a DIY video case study cost?

**$0–$300.** And I'm going to defend this option before I criticise it, because plenty of the advice you'll read online won't.

A recorded Zoom call with a happy customer, local recording turned on so you're not stuck with a compressed cloud file, is a genuinely usable asset. Phones shoot 4K. A $60 lavalier mic fixes the only thing that makes amateur video unwatchable, which is bad audio. Nobody has ever bounced off a customer video because the colour grade was flat. They bounce because they can't hear it. So if you're testing whether customer video moves anything before you spend real money, do this. A rough video of a real customer saying something specific beats a polished video of them saying nothing.

Here's where it falls over. Not the camera. The interview.

Getting someone to say the useful thing on camera is a skill, and it's the entire job. Ask "so, tell us about your experience with us" and you'll get ninety seconds of pleasant nothing: great team, really responsive, would recommend. Useless. You need the specific number, the thing that was broken before, the moment they decided, and the objection they'll now happily dismantle on your behalf. That means knowing which follow-up to ask while they're mid-sentence, and being comfortable letting a silence sit.

Then you have to edit it. Which is where DIY video really goes to die, alongside the DIY written case study that also never shipped.

## What does self-serve video collection software cost?

**$0–$249/mo**, and this tier has got genuinely good in the last two years.

The model is the same across all of them. You send your customer a link, they get a set of prompts, they record themselves on their own webcam or phone, and the software handles hosting, captions, and a widget you drop on your site. Nobody schedules anything. Nobody runs an interview.

Current pricing:

- **[Senja](https://senja.io/)**: free for 15 video and text testimonials, then Starter $29/mo and Pro $59/mo (17% off billed yearly). The most generous free tier of the lot.
- **[Testimonial.to](https://testimonial.to/)**: free (2 video, 10 text), Starter $25/mo, Ultimate $50/mo per space, Ultimate+ $95/mo per space (which adds case study collection and an AI video editor).
- **[Boast](https://boast.io/)**: Basic $59/mo, Team $119/mo, Premium $249/mo billed monthly, dropping to $50/$100/$208 on annual. Video quality is tiered, so 720p on Basic and 4K only on Premium.
- **[Vocal Video](https://vocalvideo.com/)**: free forever tier (5 videos, 20 minutes of processing a month), then Essential $99/mo, Pro $149/mo, Scale $249/mo billed yearly, with Enterprise from $1,250/mo.

For volume, this tier is unbeatable on price. If you want thirty short testimonials on a wall-of-love page, paying a studio thirty times is insane and paying Senja $29 a month is obviously correct.

The catch is response rate and what comes back. You've handed the hardest part of the job (getting a busy person to say something specific and quotable) to the busy person. Most won't reply. Of the ones who do, a good chunk will read the prompt off the screen in a flat voice, or record in a dark room, or ramble for four minutes without landing a single number. There's no producer to say "that was great, can you say the 40% bit again but start with the number."

Software collects testimonials. It doesn't produce case studies. Be clear which one you're buying.

## What does a freelance videographer charge?

**Roughly $1,000–$4,000 for a finished video**, and the reason that range is so wide is that most freelance video quotes are made of two separate things.

YunoJuno, a real freelancer marketplace rather than a content marketing blog, puts the [average videographer day rate at £427](https://www.yunojuno.com/freelancer-rates-job-role/videographer) in its 2026 rates report. That's the filming. Editing is nearly always quoted on top, hourly or per finished minute, and a properly cut case study video is not a two-hour edit.

So a freelance quote is usually a day rate for the shoot, plus travel if they're going to your customer, plus an editing figure, plus a stated number of revision rounds. Add them up and you land in that $1,000–$4,000 band depending on whether the shoot is remote or on-site and how experienced the person is.

What you're buying is a camera operator and an editor. What you're usually *not* buying is someone to decide which customer to feature, run the interview, or pull the story out. Plenty of excellent videographers will tell you straight that they're not interviewers. So either you run the interview while they film it, or you're paying for a beautifully shot video of someone saying pleasant nothing.

## What does a productised studio charge?

**A fixed fee, typically $2,000–$3,000.** [Ours is $2,500](/pricing/), so treat this section as the pitch it partly is.

The logic of this tier is that a customer story is the same shape every time, so the process can be a product rather than a bespoke quote. One recorded interview, run by someone who does this all week, harvested into every format you'd otherwise commission separately: the video case study, a short video testimonial for the homepage, the written case study, a pull-quote, and a social pack. One price, no credits, no discovery call to find out the number.

The real argument here isn't that the video beats what a premium agency shoots. It doesn't. It's that the marginal cost of a second format from an interview you've already paid for is close to zero, and almost nobody in this market charges you that way. Buy the pieces separately and you're at $3,000+ for the video, $1,750+ for the written study, $2,000+ for a video testimonial as its own line item, plus the social. That's $7,000 to $10,000 across three vendors and three timelines.

The honest downsides: you're trusting a small operation with no bench, the production values won't match a crew that flew in, and if you want ten videos next quarter this is the wrong tool. ## What does a full-service video production house charge?

**$10,000+ per video**, usually inside a retainer. That's an estimate rather than a published price, because production houses quote per project and effectively never publish rates.

You're paying for a crew rather than a person: director, DP, sound, lighting, and a client services layer on top. Scripted b-roll of the customer's office, multiple camera angles, motion graphics, colour grading by someone whose whole job is colour grading.

For a brand film at the top of a product launch, that spend makes sense. For a customer case study, most of it doesn't. Nobody has ever bought B2B software because the case study video had a second camera angle. They bought it because a person who looked like them described a problem that sounded like theirs and then said a number. Budget above roughly $3,000 buys prestige, which is a real thing to buy. Just be clear that's what it is.

## The costs nobody puts on the quote

Read this bit twice. The tier you pick matters less than the six things below, and none of them show up on a rate card.

**Travel and crew.** On-site shoots are the biggest swing factor in any video quote. Flights, hotel, a second pair of hands, and the day the videographer can't book anyone else because they're in transit. Some agencies absorb it through a network of local shooters. Freelancers almost always bill it at cost on top, and it can double a quote. If a price looks cheap, check whether travel is in it.

**Who runs the interview.** The difference between a $1,200 quote and a $3,000 one, and buyers consistently miss it. "Filming and editing" does not mean "interviewing." If nobody is booked to run the conversation, you're running it, which makes you the producer on top of your actual job.

**Editing rounds.** Nearly every quote includes one or two. Round three is billable, and round three is exactly when your customer's legal or comms team comes back with notes nobody could have anticipated. Ask what a round costs before you sign.

**Licensing and usage rights.** Check whether you own the footage or hold a licence to it, whether that licence expires, and whether it covers paid advertising. Some contracts let you use a video on your website but not behind ad spend, which is a horrible thing to learn after you've built the campaign.

**Whether a written asset comes out of it.** Video converts on a page and does almost nothing in search, because Google and AI tools read text. If your case study exists only as a video, a buyer researching in ChatGPT or Perplexity will never find it. Most video vendors don't write, so the written version becomes a separate commission, a separate brief, and often a separate conversation with the customer.

**Your customer's time.** More on this, because it's the big one.

## The cost that doesn't appear on any invoice

Your customer is doing you a favour. That's the actual currency here, and it's finite.

Book them once and you've spent a bit of goodwill. Book them twice, first for a video shoot and a fortnight later for a written case study interview because you used two vendors, and you've spent a lot more. Some of them will decline the second one. Politely, with a reasonable excuse, and you'll never know whether it was scheduling or whether you'd simply asked for too much.

Worse, you're now on their calendar as a company that generates work. That's a bad thing to be to your best customer, and a very bad thing to be right before a renewal conversation.

Nobody quotes for this because nobody can. It doesn't have a price. But it's why I built [the Proof Kit](/pricing/) around a single interview: one conversation, one ask, and both the video and the written case study fall out of the same recording. Not because one interview is marginally cheaper to produce, but because your customer's willingness to help is the resource you can't top up with budget.

When you're comparing quotes, count how many times each option puts your customer on a call.

## So which one should you actually buy?

**DIY** if you're testing whether customer video does anything for you at all. Buy a decent mic, record the Zoom locally, write your questions in advance. The video will be fine. The interview is the bit to worry about.

**Collection software** if you want volume and no single story has to carry weight. Thirty short clips on a wall-of-love page is a real marketing asset, and Senja's free tier gets you started for nothing. Just don't expect a case study out of it.

**A freelance videographer** if you know exactly what the video needs to say and need skilled hands to shoot and cut it. Get the day rate, the editing figure, the travel, and the included revision rounds in writing before you agree to anything.

**A productised studio** if you want one complete story done properly without running a procurement process. I'm obviously biased, but the case is simple: fixed price, the interview run for you, and every format out of one sitting rather than one asset and a to-do list.

**A premium agency** if you're a funded marketing team, video is your flagship channel, and you'll buy several a year. Testimonial Hero is the right answer to that brief and I'll happily point you at them.

**A production house** if the video is a brand asset rather than a proof asset, and looking expensive is part of the point.

Whichever you pick, [look at what a finished proof set actually contains](/examples/) before you spend anything. Comparing prices without comparing deliverables is how people end up paying $3,000 for a video and then discovering they still need a written case study.
